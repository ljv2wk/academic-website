Current book project

Mental Health, Identity,
and Politics

When does mental health become a political identity, and how does it resemble—or differ from—other political identities? My book examines this question through the history of mental-health politics, public opinion, and survey experiments.

Central argument

Mental health has always been political, even when that history has remained outside mainstream accounts of identity and political behavior.

The book studies how definitions of mental illness have been bound up with social norms and political power, including cases where people were regarded as mentally unwell because their actions challenged prevailing authority. It then turns to the contemporary emergence of mental-health identity.

Drawing on national surveys and survey experiments, the project examines identity formation, comparisons with other health-related identities, evaluations of candidates who disclose mental-health conditions, and responses to threats directed at the group.

The research

Key components

01

Historical and political context

Tracing the changing boundary between mental-health classification, stigma, social control, and political identity.

02

Who identifies with mental health?

Using survey measures of self-categorization, identity importance, closeness, and alienation to understand who recognizes mental-health experience as a social and political identity.

03

Comparison across health-related identities

Comparing mental-health identity with disability and chronic physical-illness identification, including variation by demographic characteristics and political orientation.

04

Candidates and political representation

Two survey experiments investigate reactions to candidates disclosing mental-health conditions, and whether voters' own experiences and identities shape candidate evaluations.

05

Threat and political mobilization

A planned survey experiment tests whether different forms of threat toward people with mental-health conditions change emotional reactions and willingness to engage politically, including responses to proposed medication restrictions and coercive treatment policies.
